There is a point in most growing firms where IT stops being a favour. Somebody in the office has been the IT person on top of their real job. They reset passwords, chase the broadband provider and set up the new starter's laptop. Then the firm reaches fifteen or twenty people, and that stops working.

At that point you have two real choices. You can hire your own IT person. Or you can pay a managed IT provider to look after it for you.

We are a managed IT provider, so you know where we stand. But the honest answer is that both are right for some firms. This post is about how to tell which one you are.

## What you get with one IT person

A good hire gives you things a provider cannot. They are in the building. They know every person, every habit and every workaround. They can fix the screen in the meeting room five minutes before the client arrives. They learn your business, and over time they can improve how it works, not just keep it running.

One hire also gives you one person. That is the part firms underestimate.

- **One person takes holidays.** By law, a full-time worker on a five-day week gets at least [28 days' paid leave a year](https://www.gov.uk/holiday-entitlement-rights). Add sick days and training, and for several weeks a year nobody is looking after your systems.
- **One person knows some things well.** The person who is good with laptops and printers is not always good with firewalls, backups and Microsoft 365 security settings. One salary buys one set of skills.
- **One person leaves.** When they go, what they know goes with them. If the passwords, the network layout and the backup schedule live in their head, you find out how much when they hand in their notice.

None of this means a hire is wrong. It means a hire is a person, not a department.

## What a hire really costs

We are not going to quote you a salary. Pay for IT roles moves with the market and with the town, and a number nobody counted is no help to you. Look at three current local job adverts for the role you have in mind. That is your starting figure.

Then add what the advert does not show. Some of these costs are set by law:

- **Employer National Insurance.** For 2026 to 2027, employers pay [15% on earnings above £5,000 a year](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rates-and-thresholds-for-employers-2026-to-2027). Many small employers can claim the Employment Allowance, which is £10,500 for 2026 to 2027 and comes off the total employer National Insurance bill. Ask your accountant whether you qualify, and how much of it your other staff already use.
- **Pension.** Under automatic enrolment, the employer pays at least [3% of qualifying earnings](https://www.gov.uk/workplace-pensions/what-you-your-employer-and-the-government-pay).

Some are set by the job:

- **Recruitment.** An agency fee, or a lot of your own time.
- **Training.** Their skills have to keep up with your systems.
- **Tools.** A professional needs device management, remote support, backup and monitoring software. A provider spreads these across all its clients. A single hire needs their own.
- **Cover.** Somebody has to look after things when they are away. Often that somebody is an IT provider anyway.

Add all of it up before you compare. The salary is only the start.

## What managed IT gives you, and what it does not

A managed IT provider gives you a team instead of a person. Holidays and sick days are the provider's problem, not yours. You get a range of skills across the team, not whatever one person happens to know. The record of how your systems are set up should belong to a process, not to one person's memory. And the price is usually a monthly figure per user or per device, so it grows with your firm a little at a time, not one salary at a time.

It also has real limits. You should hear them from us before you sign with anybody.

- **A provider is not in your office every day.** Most work is done remotely. That is quick for most problems, but it is not the same as somebody at the next desk.
- **A provider does not know your business the way an employee does.** A good one learns it. A poor one treats you as a queue of tickets.
- **Not everything is covered.** Some contracts cover the help desk and nothing behind it. Ask what happens to patching, backups and user access, not just to the broken laptop.
- **A response time is not a fix time.** The time on the contract usually promises when somebody starts work on your problem, not when it is solved. Read what it actually says.

## When your own IT person is the right answer

Hire your own person if most of these are true for you:

- You have a lot of hands-on work every day: a warehouse, a shop floor, or a lot of equipment that physically breaks.
- Your systems are unusual, and knowing how they fit together is the valuable part.
- You want somebody to change how the business works, not just keep it running, and you can give them the time to do it.
- You have enough work to keep one person busy all week, and you can pay for cover when they are away.

## When managed IT is the right answer

Use a provider if most of these are true for you:

- You have between 5 and 50 people, and IT is not a full week's work.
- What you mostly need is for things to work: email, files, laptops, Microsoft 365, backups and security.
- You cannot afford for IT to stop when one person is on holiday.
- You want security and backups done by people who do them for many businesses, not learned on the job.

For most firms in that range, a provider is the better fit. That is not because a hire is a bad idea. It is because one person is not enough cover, and two people is more than the work needs.

## The middle option

You do not have to choose one. Some firms keep one person in-house who knows the business and the people, and use a provider behind them for cover, security and specialist work.

That works well, as long as everybody knows who owns what. Write it down. The gap between two parties is where problems hide.

## Questions to ask, whichever you choose

If you are hiring:

- Who covers when they are on holiday or off sick?
- Where is everything written down, and who else can read it?
- What happens to the admin passwords if they leave?

If you are talking to a provider:

- What exactly does the monthly price cover, and what costs extra?
- Does the contract cover patching, backups and user access, or only the help desk?
- Do you test that backups can be restored, or only that they ran?
- If we leave, what do we get back, and how quickly?

Any provider worth using answers all four without a pause. Ask us the same questions.
